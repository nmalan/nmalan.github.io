Why Rossby waves go west

Rossby waves are how the ocean and atmosphere adjust on large scales. In the ocean they carry the response to changing winds westward across whole basins, which is part of why the strongest currents, like the East Australian Current, run along the western edges of ocean basins. It is also why sea-level anomalies seen by satellites drift steadily west. In the atmosphere they shape the meanders of the jet stream and the weather systems that travel along it.

Rossby waves always propagate west relative to the mean flow, but the usual explanation is hard to picture. Textbooks push a chain of fluid parcels north and south and work out the vorticity each parcel gains or loses. They then infer the velocities that shift the pattern west. That takes several steps, all in a frame where the wave keeps moving.

Schneider (2015) gives a simpler view: ride along with the wave. In that frame each parcel's path is fixed, and asking which way parcels can pass through it gives the westward propagation directly.

The argument

Move with the wave at its phase speed c. The pattern stops, the flow is steady, and streamlines become trajectories. Parcels pass through at ū − c: from the west if this is positive, from the east if negative.

Absolute vorticity is conserved following a parcel. A parcel displaced north by δy gains planetary vorticity, so its relative vorticity must fall by the same amount:

δζ = −β δy(1)

Northward means clockwise, .

Now follow that spin through. A parcel moving east that spins clockwise turns right, which is back toward the south. The spin PV demands pulls displaced parcels back to their rest latitude, and this restoring tendency holds the wave together. A parcel moving west also turns right, but for it right is north, so the spin pushes it further from its rest latitude and no wave can form. These are the turns drawn in Fig. 2a.

When the wave has no north–south structure, a parcel's relative vorticity is set entirely by how sharply its path curves. Heading east over a crest, it turns right; heading west, it turns left. From the streamfunction,

δζ = −K²(ū − c) δy(2)

Equations (1) and (2) describe the same parcel, so they must agree. The signs match only when ū − c > 0. Parcels must therefore enter from the west, and the wave moves west relative to the flow. Matching the magnitudes as well gives the dispersion relation,

c = ū − β / K²(3)

The displacement amplitude cancels, so the result holds for any small wave. Schneider (2015) extends the argument to waves with north–south structure and to baroclinic waves that propagate vertically. Viewed from the ground (Fig. 2b) the same wave is much harder to read. The pattern moves while parcels drift at ū, so streamlines and trajectories no longer coincide. With no mean flow a parcel only bobs north and south as the crests pass.

Fig. 2c shows what the wave does below the surface. In the ocean, long Rossby waves are mostly baroclinic. Their flow is carried by heave of the thermocline, which is deep under anticyclonic flow and shallow under cyclonic flow, while the sea surface bulges the opposite way by a few centimetres. A mooring sees the thermocline rise and fall as the wave passes. Panels (a) and (b) show a barotropic wave. For a baroclinic one, vortex stretching joins the PV balance and slows the wave, but the westward result is unchanged (Schneider 2015, section 3).
